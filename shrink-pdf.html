    How to shrink a PDF to an exact size

    1. Drop in the PDF

      One file at a time. The page count and original size appear immediately so you know what you are working with.

    2. Pick the ceiling you have to fit under

      Use a preset — 5, 10, or 25 MB — or type any number, including decimals such as 4.5. Aim slightly below the real limit: mail servers measure the whole encoded message, not just your attachment.

    3. Let it search, or take the controls

      Target mode tries a high-quality setting first and steps down only as far as it must. If you would rather decide yourself, switch on Manual quality instead and set JPEG quality and maximum page resolution directly.

    4. Open the result before you delete the original

      Check the pages that matter — small print, stamps, signatures, fine tables. If anything is too soft, raise the target by a megabyte or two and run it again.

    What the tool actually does to your pages

    Each page is rendered exactly as a PDF reader would display it, then that rendering is saved as a JPEG image and placed on a page of the original dimensions. The document keeps its page count, page sizes, and visual layout. What it loses is everything that was structured data rather than appearance:

    • Text becomes pixels. You cannot select, copy, or search it, and screen readers can no longer read it aloud.
    • Links, bookmarks, and form fields disappear, because they are annotations attached to text, not part of the visible page.
    • Fine detail softens. Hairlines, small serif type, and subtle gradients suffer first; photographs cope well.
    • File size becomes predictable, which is the entire point — it is now governed by resolution and JPEG quality rather than by the document's contents.

    That trade is right for a scanned lease you need to email today, and wrong for a contract someone will search through next year. When accessibility or searchability matters, lossless compression or splitting the document is the better answer.

    How target mode chooses its settings

    There is no formula that turns "10 MB" into a quality number, because it depends on what is on the pages. Instead, the tool works through a ladder of presets ordered from best to smallest — each one a pairing of maximum page resolution and JPEG quality — and performs a binary search over it, re-rendering the document at each step it tests.

    The result is the highest-quality preset on the ladder that still lands under your target, usually found in three or four passes rather than by testing every rung. If even the smallest preset overshoots, you get that smallest version anyway, clearly labelled, so you can decide whether to split the document instead.

    Common limits worth targeting

    Attachment limits are quoted for the encoded message, and email encoding inflates a binary file by roughly a third. A "25 MB limit" therefore accepts an attachment of around 18 MB in practice. The right-hand column is the target to set here.

    Limits change and administrators override defaults, so treat these as starting points rather than guarantees. When a portal states a limit, target roughly 20% below it.

    Setting quality by hand

    Manual mode exposes the two levers that matter. Resolution is the stronger of the two: halving it removes about three quarters of the pixels, while dropping JPEG quality from 80 to 60 typically saves a third.

    SettingBest forWhat suffers
    2000–2400 px · quality 85Archival scans, anything to be reprintedVery little; savings are modest
    1600 px · quality 75The everyday default — email and portalsSlight softening of very small type
    1200 px · quality 65Screen reading and quick reviewFootnotes and 6pt text get fuzzy
    800–1000 px · quality 45Hard limits you cannot negotiateVisible artefacts; body text still legible

    A note on printing

    A page rendered at 1600 px across A4 works out to roughly 195 DPI — fine on screen, acceptable on an office printer, short of the 300 DPI that commercial printing expects. If the document will be printed professionally, keep the original and send it as a link.

    Frequently asked questions

    Are my files really private?

    Yes. Pages are rendered and re-encoded inside your browser tab using your device's own graphics pipeline. Nothing is uploaded, stored, or transmitted, and the data is discarded when you close the tab.

    Why can't I select the text any more?

    Because each page has been replaced by an image of itself. That is how the large savings are achieved. If you need selectable text, use the lossless compressor instead, or keep the original alongside the shrunken copy.

    The result is still above my target. What now?

    The tool reached the smallest preset it has and reports that clearly. Options: split the document into two files, remove pages you do not need, or crop large blank margins before shrinking. For scans, converting to greyscale in your scanner software before re-scanning also helps a great deal.

    How long does it take?

    Roughly a second per page per pass on a modern laptop, and target mode makes three or four passes. A 40-page document is usually done in well under a minute; a 300-page one is worth starting and leaving alone.

    Does it work on scanned documents?

    Yes — that is where it performs best. Scans are already images, so re-rendering them at a sensible resolution often cuts 80–95% of the file size with little visible difference on screen.

    Will the page count or page size change?

    No. Every page keeps its position and dimensions. Only the content of each page is replaced with a rendered image.

    Can I undo it?

    Not from the shrunken file — the discarded detail is gone. Always keep the original until you have checked the output, and download the result under a new name (the tool adds a suffix for exactly this reason).
